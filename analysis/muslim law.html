Part I: Islamic Jurisprudence (Usul al-Fiqh)

A thorough understanding of the foundational principles of Islamic jurisprudence is essential, as these concepts are consistently tested in both multiple-choice and essay questions.

1.1 Primary and Secondary Sources of Islamic Law

Islamic law is derived from a hierarchy of sources that provide the basis for all legal rulings.1

  • Primary Sources:
    • Quran: The Holy Book of Muslims, considered the direct and unaltered word of God. It is the supreme and primary source of Islamic law.
    • Sunnah: The traditions, practices, sayings, and approvals of the Prophet Muhammad. The Sunnah is recorded in collections of Hadith and is considered of equal importance to Quranic injunctions for deriving legal principles.
  • Secondary Sources:
    • Ijma (Consensus): The unanimous agreement of qualified Islamic legal scholars on a specific issue after the death of the Prophet. It is used when the Quran and Sunnah do not provide an explicit rule. In the modern context, the role of a legislative assembly in achieving Ijma is a subject of debate.
    • Qiyas (Analogical Reasoning): This involves extending a known legal ruling from an original case to a new one because both share the same effective cause ('illah). For example, the prohibition of wine due to its intoxicating property is extended to all intoxicants through Qiyas.

1.2 Principles of Legal Reasoning

Beyond the main sources, several principles of legal reasoning are frequently examined.

  • Ijtihad (Independent Legal Reasoning): This is the effort a jurist makes to derive a legal ruling when the primary sources are not explicit. A person qualified to perform Ijtihad is called a 'Mujtahid'.
  • Istihsan (Juristic Preference): Literally meaning "to consider something good," Istihsan allows a jurist to depart from a ruling derived from strict analogy (Qiyas) in favor of a different ruling that is considered more just or serves the public interest (maslahah). This is a key principle in the Hanafi school but is rejected by the Shafi'i school, which views it as arbitrary.
  • Naskh (Abrogation): This is the principle where one Shari'ah ruling is suspended or replaced by a later one from the Quran or Sunnah. This doctrine is used to resolve apparent contradictions and is understood as a divine process to adapt laws to the evolving needs of the community.
  • Maqasid al-Shari'ah (Objectives of Islamic Law): This refers to the higher objectives or purposes behind Islamic rulings, which aim to enhance public good (maslaha) and prevent harm. The five core protections are faith (din), life (nafs), lineage (nasl), intellect ('aql), and property (mal). This framework is crucial for applying Islamic law in modern contexts.

1.3 Schools of Jurisprudence (Madhahib)

The exams consistently test knowledge of the major Sunni schools of thought (madhhab).

  • Hanafi School: Founded by Imam Abu Hanifa, this school is predominant in South and Central Asia, including Pakistan. It is known for its reliance on Qiyas and Istihsan in its legal reasoning.
  • Shafi'i School: Founded by Imam al-Shafi'i, this school is prevalent in Southeast Asia and parts of the Middle East. It emphasizes the Quran and Sunnah and rejects Istihsan as a valid source of law. Part II: Muslim Family Law in Pakistan

This area is heavily tested, with a focus on the practical application of Islamic principles and the impact of Pakistani legislation.

2.1 Marriage and Divorce

  • Nature of Marriage: Under Muslim law, marriage (Nikah) is considered a civil contract, requiring an offer (Ijab) and acceptance (Qabul). The marriage must be registered under the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, 1961 (MFLO) to be legally valid in Pakistan.
  • Dissolution of Marriage:
    • Talaq (Divorce by Husband): This is the husband's right to dissolve the marriage. The MFLO 1961 regulates this process, requiring a written notice to the local Union Council Chairman and a 90-day reconciliation period before the divorce becomes effective.
    • Khula (Divorce by Wife): This is the wife's right to seek dissolution of the marriage through the courts. A woman can file for Khula on various grounds, including cruelty, failure to provide maintenance, or simply stating that she can no longer live with her husband "within the limits prescribed by Allah". This right is primarily governed by the Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act, 1939. Unlike Talaq, Khula often requires the wife to return her dower (Mehr).

2.2 The Muslim Family Laws Ordinance (MFLO), 1961

The MFLO is a critical piece of legislation that introduced significant reforms to classical Muslim family law in Pakistan. It is a high-frequency exam topic.

  • Polygamy (Section 6): A man cannot contract a second marriage without written permission from an Arbitration Council, which must be satisfied that the proposed marriage is necessary and just, and that the consent of the existing wife (or wives) has been obtained.
  • Inheritance for Orphaned Grandchildren (Section 4): This is one of the most controversial provisions. It grants children of a predeceased son or daughter the right to inherit the share their parent would have received if alive. This principle of "representational succession" deviates from the classical Islamic rule that a nearer heir excludes a more remote one. The Federal Shariat Court declared this section un-Islamic, but it remains a contentious and frequently tested legal issue.

2.3 Inheritance and Property Transfer

  • General Principles of Inheritance: Islamic law sets out strict rules for the distribution of a deceased person's estate. The process requires first paying for funeral expenses and clearing all debts, including any unpaid dower (Haq Mehr).
  • Wasiyyat (Will): A person can bequeath up to one-third of their property through a will. Under Sunni law, a bequest to a legal heir is not valid unless other heirs consent.
  • Hiba (Gift): A Hiba is a transfer of property made during one's lifetime without consideration. Unlike a will, there is no limit on the amount of property that can be gifted. The essential elements are declaration (ijab), acceptance (qabul), and delivery of possession (qabza).

2.4 Custody of Minors (Hizanat)

The rules for custody are governed by both Islamic principles and the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890.

  • Right of Hizanat: This refers to the mother's right to the physical custody of her children. Under Hanafi law, which is followed in Pakistan, this right extends until the age of seven for a male child and until puberty for a female child.
  • Father's Role as Guardian: The father is considered the natural and legal guardian (wilayah), responsible for the child's overall welfare, including financial support, regardless of who has custody.
  • Paramount Consideration: The ultimate deciding factor in all custody cases is the welfare of the child. Courts in Pakistan can deviate from the strict rules of Hizanat if it is in the child's best interest.

Part III: Islamic Criminal Law

Islamic criminal jurisprudence categorizes offenses into three main types, each with distinct punishments and standards of proof.

  • Hadd: These are crimes "against God" with punishments fixed in the Quran and Sunnah. Examples include theft, illicit sexual intercourse (zina), and false accusation of zina (qadhf). The punishments are severe (e.g., amputation, flogging) and cannot be pardoned.
  • Qisas: This refers to "an eye for an eye" retaliation for crimes against an individual, such as murder or bodily harm. The victim or their heirs have the right to demand equal retribution or to forgive the offender, often in exchange for monetary compensation (Diyya).
  • Tazir: These are discretionary punishments for offenses not covered by Hadd or Qisas. The judge or ruler has the authority to determine the punishment, which can range from a warning to imprisonment or flogging.

Part IV: Contemporary Issues and Modern Applications

4.1 Islamization of Laws in Pakistan

The process of aligning Pakistan's legal framework with Islamic principles is a recurring theme in jurisprudence exams.

  • Historical Context: The process began with the Objectives Resolution of 1949, which declared that all laws must conform to the injunctions of Islam.
  • Zia-ul-Haq's Era (1977-1988): This period saw the most significant phase of Islamization, with the introduction of the Hudood Ordinances (1979) and the establishment of the Federal Shariat Court to review laws for repugnancy to Islam.
  • Post-Zia Reforms: Subsequent governments have grappled with this legacy, with some laws being reformed, such as the Women's Protection Bill of 2006, which aimed to moderate the effects of the Hudood Ordinances.
